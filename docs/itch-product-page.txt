I've been interested in evolutionary computing for quite a while - in fact, I did my master's degree in Evolutionary and Adaptive Systems at Sussex University.

I came across the evolutionary art of William Latham (https://www.gold.ac.uk/computing/people/w-latham/) ages ago. This project is an homage to one of his art pieces.

Morphogen lets you breed strange, organic 3D sculptures - horns, shells, flowers, crowns and creatures - without modelling anything yourself. You don't design the forms; you choose the ones you like, and evolution does the rest.


FEATURES

- Evolve 3D forms by aesthetic selection: you are the fitness function. Pick the creation you like best and a new generation is bred around it.
- A 3x3 grid of live, fully interactive 3D views: the current creation in the centre, surrounded by eight mutants. Rotate, zoom and pan any of them, or open one full screen.
- Re-roll a generation you don't like, or step back to undo a selection.
- Sexual reproduction: keep your favourite creations in a project, then cross any of them with the current creation to breed offspring that mix features of both parents.
- Fused, organic surfaces: spheres can melt smoothly into one another, from a slight web between beads to a single flowing body - and different parts of a creation can fuse to different degrees.
- Evolved colour textures and bump maps: procedural patterns (fractal noise, cells, bands and spirals, warped, blended and masked together) that evolve along with the shape. They wrap seamlessly around every surface.
- Evolving materials: colour, roughness, metalness and clear coat are all part of the genome.
- High quality real-time rendering: physically based materials, studio reflections, shadows, ambient occlusion and anti-aliasing, all on the GPU.
- Export high-resolution images (PNG, JPEG or TIFF, up to 16384 pixels per side) from any angle.
- Save whole projects, or import and export single genomes as human-readable files to share with others.
- Control how wild evolution gets: adjust the mutation strength and limits on the number of spheres, textures and bump maps.
- Light and dark themes.


HOW IT WORKS

Every creation is grown from a genome. Unlike the linear strings of classic genetic algorithms, a Morphogen genome is a tree:

- Spheres are the leaves and the basic building blocks. Each has a size, a shininess, and either a plain colour or a texture.
- Groups combine several sub-structures.
- Linear repeats copy their contents over and over, moving, turning and shrinking a little each time. This produces rows, arcs, spirals and tapering horns.
- Radial repeats copy their contents around an axis, giving rings, flowers and crowns.

Each node can also shift the colour, size and fusion of everything beneath it, and repeats can compound that shift with every step, so a horn might fade from red to gold, or be solid at its base and beaded at its tip. Repeats nested inside repeats are where the really organic shapes come from.

To develop a creation, the genome is grown into a set of spheres in 3D space. Spheres that fuse are treated as smooth distance fields that blend into one another, and are turned into a single mesh. Textures and bump maps are themselves small trees of image operations - patterns, filters and combiners - which are baked into images and wrapped onto the surfaces.

Mutation makes both small and large changes. Small changes nudge numbers: a slightly bigger sphere, a few more repeats, a tighter spiral, a shifted hue. Large changes alter the structure: wrapping part of the creation in a new repeat, removing or duplicating branches, swapping one kind of repeat for another, or adding and reshaping textures. Crossover takes a copy of one parent and grafts in a branch of the other, along with any textures it uses.

Then it's up to you. Each generation you pick the one that catches your eye, and over a few dozen generations the forms drift in directions no one planned.


REQUIREMENTS

Windows, with a Direct3D 11 capable graphics card.
